# studying

A study document has one reader: Eric, alone, without this conversation. A cheat sheet is read
cold mid-exam. A study guide is read days later. Write each line for that reader.

## Before drafting

1. Pin the task in a `README.md` in the exam's folder, from Eric's words: which exam, its date,
   the scope source (syllabus, lecture list, exam announcement), the format limits (sheets,
   sides, handwritten or typed, allowed materials), and everything he said to include. Show it
   and start only after he confirms. Reread it before each new draft
2. Read the scope source before choosing content. Content comes from what the course taught:
   its lectures, notes, problem sets, and past exams. An item from a textbook chapter, a review
   packet, or general knowledge goes in only after you find it in the course material; say so
   when you cannot
3. A remark about what Eric knows well sets pacing, never a cut. Propose any cut of content he
   asked for, with what is lost, and wait

## Writing

- Each line says what it is for and gives the full notation, as the course writes it
- Use the course's terms in the course's sense. Define a term once, in the document itself
- Cite sources by names Eric can find on the course site (lecture title and date, problem set
  number), never by an internal file name or page of a scraped copy
- Order by how he will look things up during the exam, not by the source's order

## Ready check

The math being right is not enough. Before calling a document ready, give it to a fresh subagent
with no context and the exam's `README.md`, ask it to list every line it cannot decode or could
not use mid-exam, and fix those. Report what that check found.

Practice problems follow the `socratic-tutor` skill when tutoring, and `hw-explain` in the LaTeX
repo for one problem: say what a problem tests before working its parts.
